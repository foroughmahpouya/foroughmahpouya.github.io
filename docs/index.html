Forough Mahpouya

Ph.D. Candidate in Industrial and Systems Engineering

Department of Industrial and Systems Engineering · University at Buffalo

I develop causal learning methods for evaluating decisions with observational data, with healthcare as my primary application. My research asks whether interventions actually improve outcomes, for whom they work, and how reliable conclusions can be drawn when the people receiving different interventions differ before treatment.

My methodological work focuses on balancing representation learning for continuous interventions, repeated decisions, and time-to-event outcomes. I also study treatment-effect heterogeneity and sequential experimentation—how to decide which comparison, experiment, or review would provide the most useful additional evidence.

My future research extends these ideas to human-AI decision systems. I am interested in how hospitals should evaluate AI systems after deployment, when recommendations begin to influence clinician decisions and patient outcomes, and when additional human review is necessary. I will also extend this work to AI-assisted quality inspection and other safety-critical systems.

My work has appeared in IISE Transactions and Quality and Reliability Engineering International. I received the 2025–2026 University at Buffalo ISE Graduate Student Teaching Award and was selected as an IISE Future Faculty Fellow.

My research develops causal learning and decision methods for observational data, with a primary focus on healthcare. I combine causal inference, representation learning, and sequential experimentation to study whether interventions improve outcomes, for whom they work, and what additional evidence would be most useful for decision-making.

Causal Learning from Observational Health Data

I study how intervention definition, comparison groups, and measurement affect the conclusions that can be drawn from observational data. My healthcare work spans dementia management, caregiving, and cardiometabolic health and includes research on propensity-score study quality, treatment-effect heterogeneity, and adversarial balancing methods.

A central focus of this work is balancing representation learning, which transforms measured characteristics so that people receiving different interventions become more comparable. One line of my dissertation develops a theoretical neural framework for continuous interventions and time-to-event outcomes, including identification conditions and connections to hazard modeling. In a separate applied study, I develop and evaluate continuous-treatment balancing losses and use them to estimate how caregiver burden affects the probability of long-term-care transition.

Sequential Experimentation and Targeted Evaluation

I also study how to decide what information should be collected next when every possible comparison cannot be observed. In work published in IISE Transactions, my coauthors and I developed a sequential experimentation method that selects pairwise comparisons based on their expected value for identifying top-ranked alternatives.

I am extending this idea to targeted evaluation of AI-assisted decisions, where limited human review can be directed toward cases most likely to reveal an important failure or resolve consequential uncertainty.

Human-AI Decision Systems

My future research will study how AI systems should be evaluated after deployment, when their recommendations begin to influence human decisions and outcomes. I plan to begin with deterioration alerts for hospitalized patients, studying whether alerts improve care, which patients benefit, how clinician responses affect outcomes, and when human confirmation is needed.

I will extend this framework to other AI-supported clinical decisions and to AI-assisted quality inspection and predictive maintenance.

I believe good teaching begins with listening to how students reason and designing activities that make that reasoning visible. In Simulation Modeling and Analysis, I have led recitations and course lectures and developed case-based exercises using Arena and @RISK in which students define systems, identify sources of uncertainty, make modeling choices, and explain what their results do and do not justify. These contributions, together with my broader work in the course, were recognized with the University at Buffalo ISE Graduate Student Teaching Award.
